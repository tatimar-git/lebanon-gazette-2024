Dry food indoor using air
Air drying differs from sun-drying since it takes place indoor, in a well-ventilated room or screened-in porch. Herbs, peppers and mushrooms are the most common air-dried foods.
Pros
- Free with no cost
- No need to pay for any equipment
- No energy consumption
- Easy to apply
Cons
- Humidity may increase before food dries, causing moulds
- May produce inconsistent drying, the perimeter might dry out well while in the middle food is still mushy and sometimes mouldy
- Colour and flavour fades as food dries
- It takes a lot of time, up to 2 weeks
1) Drying herbs
Less tender herbs such as thyme, rosemary, sage, oregano and parsley are most suitable for the air-drying method.
- Tie herbs in small bunches
- Hang them to dry for around 2 weeks until dry
- Cut the string
- Strip off the dried herbs from the stalk
- Place the dried herbs in an airtight container (jar)
2) Drying peppers
- Wash and dry the peppers
- Thread the needle with a strong cooking twine
- Run the needle through just above the base in the middle and push it through
- Carry on doing the same with the rest of the peppers
- Try to make strings of 20 even sized peppers
- Hang strings in a dry and dark room for around 6 weeks to dry
• Store dried peppers in an airtight container
